## 1) Raw rant (verbatim, lightly cleaned for readability)

I feel like this course needs to open up with a statement of encouragement but a reality check: it is hard work — but if they persist, they can do it. Knowledge is a gap, and creating that gap starts here.

Customers are a pain in the butt sometimes. We don’t operate on a “the customer is always right” policy — but (huge but) we calculate the value of making them upset: “Is a negative Google review really worth not cleaning out their gutter that was not part of the scope?”

The vast majority of stains/tasks can be completed with a few chemicals: SH, acid (oxalic and muriatic), and degreaser. Yes, there are some other chemicals we use for oxidation removal and gutter cleaning (LA’s Totally Awesome for gutters, EBC/Enviro Bio Cleaner for oxidation removal).

Never use a chemical on a surface if you are unsure.

Always, always test painted surfaces for pigment/finish risk. You will see it fail and change color almost immediately. Do it in an inconspicuous area. A lot of the time it’s blues (especially dark blues) and tans. But test everything — because it’s either that, or risk thousands (upwards of tens of thousands) to repaint an entire house.

I feel like the natural progression with roof cleaning is to start out with a 12-volt, try it. If you do a few decent-sized roof jobs, you can buy yourself an air compressor and an AODD pump (my preference). Preference matters — I don’t care about shooting 50 feet and I don’t care about putting out 5 gallons per minute.

Another rule of thumb for guys starting out: you don’t need everything to start. Whatever you can’t afford, rent it. Your money is better spent getting leads early on instead of having a bunch of obscure tools of the trade that are only used once in a while.

Getting leads early is hard. I don’t want to go into detail over the basic stuff (door knocking, yard signs, etc.). However, I want to emphasize that in today’s age, SEO — and even more so in my opinion, GEO — is where the marketing dollars need to go.

Answer your phone, answer your phone, answer your phone. I can’t tell you how many jobs we landed just because we were the first one to pick up.

- Build tools in a progression; don’t overbuy early.

### Insert F — “Customer value lens (reviews vs scope)” (ops + closeout)
We don’t run on “the customer is always right.” But we do run on math.

Ask yourself: is a negative Google review really worth refusing a small fix that takes five minutes — even if it wasn’t in the original scope?

### Insert G — “The simple chemical toolkit” (chemicals module)
Most stains and tasks are handled with a short list: SH, a couple acids (oxalic and muriatic), and a degreaser.

The trick isn’t owning 30 bottles — it’s knowing when to *not* touch a surface until you’ve tested it.

### Insert H — “Test before you touch” (houses/paint risk)
If you’re unsure, don’t guess.

Test painted surfaces in an inconspicuous spot. Some paints (often dark blues and tans) can fail fast. Testing is cheap. Repainting an entire house is not.

### Insert I — “Progression mindset (rent gear, reinvest)” (getting started)
You don’t need everything to start.

Rent what you can’t afford, reinvest off real jobs, and put early money into leads — not obscure tools you’ll use twice a year.

### Insert J — “Answer the phone” (marketing)
Speed-to-lead is a superpower.

Answer your phone. You’ll land jobs simply because you were first to pick up.
