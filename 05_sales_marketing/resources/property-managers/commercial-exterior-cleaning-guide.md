# Commercial Exterior Cleaning Guide (For Property Managers)

If you manage a commercial property, exterior cleaning isn’t “just cosmetic.” It affects:
- tenant retention
- customer perception
- slip-and-fall risk
- surface lifespan
- compliance exposure (especially runoff and stormwater)

This guide explains what to clean, how often to schedule it, what documentation to expect, and how to avoid the common vendor mistakes that create damage and liability.

---

## 1) What commercial exterior cleaning includes (in real life)
Most commercial programs include a mix of **soft washing** and **pressure washing**, depending on the surface.

### Flatwork (pressure washing lane)
- sidewalks
- entry pads
- breezeways
- dumpster pads
- loading areas
- drive lanes (where applicable)

**Goal:** reduce slip hazards and restore curb appeal without etching concrete or blowing out joints.

### Building exterior (soft wash lane)
- stucco, painted walls, soffits
- trim, signage areas (surface-dependent)

**Goal:** remove organic growth and grime using low-pressure methods that reduce the risk of damage.

### Specialty zones (surface + risk dependent)
- drive-thrus
- grease-heavy zones
- gum removal
- graffiti (case-by-case)

---

## 2) Scheduling: how often should a property be cleaned?
There is no single answer. The right schedule depends on:
- foot traffic
- shade + tree cover
- nearby irrigation overspray
- tenant type (restaurant vs office)
- how fast the property shows stains

### A simple baseline (start here)
- **Sidewalks + entries:** monthly to quarterly
- **Dumpster pads:** monthly (more often for food service)
- **Building exterior:** 1–2x per year (more if shaded/humid)
- **Parking/drive lanes:** 2–4x per year (higher for restaurants)

If you want it dialed in, the best approach is a walk-through + photo set + a written plan.

---

## 3) The real value: liability and documentation
If someone slips, the first question becomes: *what did you do to maintain safe conditions?*

A professional cleaning program includes:
- a recurring schedule
- pre/post photos
- notes on exceptions/limitations
- documented communication

This doesn’t just keep the property clean — it protects you when there’s a complaint.

---

## 4) Compliance basics (plain language)
Runoff matters.

If a vendor can’t explain how they prevent dirty water / wash water from becoming a stormwater problem, that’s a red flag.

A legitimate provider should be able to explain:
- how they manage runoff and sensitive areas
- how they handle higher-risk zones (oil/grease areas)
- what their standard operating procedure is for “do not discharge” conditions

---

## 5) How to choose a vendor (what to ask)
Here’s what separates a professional commercial operator from a crew with a machine.

### Non-negotiables
- proof of insurance (COI)
- surface-appropriate methods (soft wash vs pressure wash boundaries)
- a plan for runoff / stormwater concerns
- a clear scope (what is included and excluded)
- documentation (before/after + notes)

### Red flags
- quote without walking the property
- no documentation
- no clear method explanation (everything is “just pressure washing”)
- pricing far below market with no explanation

---

## 6) What you should receive after service (closeout)
A professional closeout should include:
- before/after photos
- notes on limitations and exceptions
- confirmation the job is complete
- a follow-up channel if you see an issue

---

## Bottom line
Commercial cleaning works best as a program, not a one-time emergency.

If you want the property to look maintained (and stay that way), schedule it, document it, and treat cleaning as risk control — not a last-minute cosmetic fix.
